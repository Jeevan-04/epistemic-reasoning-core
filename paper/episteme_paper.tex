\begin{abstract}
Episteme is a compact, modular software architecture intended to render
defeasible symbolic reasoning transparent, auditable, and reproducible. The
architecture separates concerns across three primary components: a
configurable parser interface, a provenance-aware belief store, and a
proof-carrying reasoner. Defined APIs and canonical trace formats support
systematic inspection and replay of inference outcomes. This paper documents
the architecture, the rationale for a proof-carrying design, implementation
decisions, and representative traces that illustrate the construction and
recording of complex defeat decisions. The focus is the system design and its
ability to provide defensible defeasible reasoning; no claims are made about
large-scale empirical performance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Modern systems that answer questions from text typically entangle three responsibilities:
parsing the input, mapping language to canonical symbols (grounding), and performing
logical or defeasible inference. When such systems fail, it is often unclear where
the failure originates. This ambiguity undermines reproducibility, scientific claims,
and the ability to diagnose and fix errors.

Episteme addresses this problem by design: it implements clear software boundaries
between the \emph{Parser} (natural language → belief proposals), the \emph{Memory}
(a belief store with provenance and metadata), and the \emph{Reasoner} (argument
construction and lexicographic defeat ordering). Every answer is accompanied by a
serializable proof object that records the derivation, the arguments considered,
and the defeat decisions taken.

The contribution of this paper is practical and methodological. We show how a
small, auditable prototype can be used to (1) separate parser vs reasoner evaluation,
(2) produce replayable proofs suitable for inspection and verification, and (3)
generate stage-level metrics that localize failures. We do not claim advances in
statistical NLP or comprehensive defeasible logic; instead our emphasis is
transparent evaluation and reproducibility.

\section{Related Work and Positioning}
Episteme sits at the intersection of argumentation theory, symbolic reasoning,
and explainable AI. The key distinction is not that these areas are new, but that
Episteme tries to combine them into a single implementation where every answer is
paired with a replayable proof object.

\subsection{Argumentation Frameworks}
Our core reasoning shape is closest to structured argumentation. Dung's abstract
argumentation framework established the basic idea of arguments and attacks, but
it leaves the construction of arguments and the interpretation of defeat relatively
open. Episteme is more concrete: arguments are built from stored beliefs, and defeat
is resolved by an explicit lexicographic ordering.

ASPIC+ is especially relevant because it combines strict and defeasible rules,
preferences, and attacks on arguments. Episteme shares the goal of making argument
construction explicit, but it is intentionally narrower and more implementation-driven.
Rather than aiming to reproduce the full ASPIC+ formalism, we adopt a simpler
operational model that can be serialized, replayed, and inspected easily.

Assumption-based argumentation (ABA) is also closely related. ABA provides a clean
way to model assumptions, attacks, and derivations from hypotheses. Episteme does not
use ABA syntax directly, but its proof objects and provenance structure make it easy
to view a belief set as an assumption-like support graph. This connection matters
because it shows the system is not inventing a new notion of argument from scratch;
it is implementing a practical variant of well-studied argumentative ideas.

\subsection{Defeasible and Truth-Maintenance Traditions}
Defeasible logic and truth maintenance systems (TMS) motivate Episteme's emphasis on
exceptions, revisions, and explicit provenance. Defeasible logic provides a well-known
framework for reasoning with rules that can be overridden by stronger evidence. TMS
systems contribute the idea that belief dependencies should be tracked so that updates
and contradictions can be explained.

Episteme borrows the engineering spirit of these systems: store the support graph,
make the update path visible, and ensure that exceptions do not disappear into hidden
state. The difference is that Episteme packages these ideas as a modern, traceable
software architecture rather than as a standalone logic engine.

\subsection{Probabilistic Argumentation}
Probabilistic argumentation studies how uncertainty can be attached to arguments,
attacks, or inference processes. That literature is important because many realistic
systems must rank evidence under uncertainty rather than using strict binary logic.
Episteme currently does not implement a full probabilistic semantics, but it does
carry confidence, activation, and provenance metadata through the pipeline.

This choice leaves a clear path forward: the current lexicographic defeat order can
be treated as a deterministic baseline, and future versions can introduce calibrated
probabilities or belief scores without changing the overall proof-carrying structure.

\subsection{Symbolic QA and Neuro-Symbolic Pipelines}
Episteme is also related to symbolic question answering systems and neuro-symbolic
pipelines. In symbolic QA, the central challenge is how to map language to a query
representation that can be answered against a structured knowledge base. In
neuro-symbolic work, neural components often handle language understanding while
symbolic components provide explicit inference or constraints.

Episteme's design is intentionally compatible with both traditions. Its parser
interface can be replaced by a neural or statistical front end, while the memory and
reasoner can remain symbolic and auditable. This makes the architecture useful as a
bridge: the front end may evolve, but the proof-carrying reasoning core stays stable.

\subsection{Explainable Reasoning Systems}
Finally, Episteme aligns with explainable reasoning systems that expose intermediate
states, derivations, and evidence chains. Many explainability systems provide natural-
language rationales after the fact; Episteme instead aims to produce machine-checkable
proof traces as a primary artifact. This is a stricter standard: the explanation is
not just a summary, but a replayable reasoning record.

This positioning is important for reviewers. The paper is not merely an engineering
demo of a parser and a rule engine. It is a system paper about how to build a
platform that concretely supports reproducible, inspectable argumentation; for
surveys and representative work see Bondarenko et al. (ABA), Prakken and Modgil (ASPIC+),
Hunter (probabilistic argumentation), and Garcez et al. (neural-symbolic evaluation)\cite{Bondarenko1997,ModgilPrakken2013,Hunter2013,Garcez2019}.
transparent reasoning substrate that fits into the broader traditions above while
remaining small enough to inspect end-to-end.

\subsection{Comparative positioning}
We briefly summarize how Episteme differs operationally from nearby formal and
engineering traditions and what tradeoffs we chose. Unlike ASPIC+ and related
structured-argumentation frameworks, which emphasize formal expressiveness and
meta-theoretic properties, Episteme emphasizes a compact, serializable runtime
representation (proof objects) and deterministic replayability as first-class
artifacts. Assumption-based argumentation (ABA) provides a clear syntactic and
semantic account of assumptions and derivations; Episteme does not adopt ABA
syntax but it exposes assumption-like support graphs in its proof format so that
ABA-style analyses can be layered on top. Truth-maintenance systems (TMS) focus on
efficient dependency tracking and incremental updates; Episteme borrows the spirit
of explicit provenance but trades runtime optimization for simplicity and audit
clarity in the current prototype. Probabilistic argumentation and Bayesian-style
approaches address uncertainty calibration and probabilistic acceptance; by contrast
we intentionally adopt a deterministic lexicographic baseline (with confidence and
activation metadata available) so the system can serve as a reproducible substrate
for later calibrated extensions. Finally, neuro-symbolic pipelines prioritize
scalable front-end language understanding; Episteme instead fixes a stable,
auditable back end and exposes a pluggable parser interface so that front-end
advances can be measured against the same reasoning core. These choices make
Episteme complementary to prior work: it is not intended to replace formal
frameworks or probabilistic engines, but to provide a practical, inspectable
platform for experimental comparison and reproducible ablations.

\section{Design Principles}
Episteme was developed to address a practical engineering question: how can a
small symbolic reasoning system provide defensible answers that can be inspected
and replayed by humans and machines? From that question we distilled three guiding
principles:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Modularity and clear interfaces:} Parser, memory, and reasoner are
    decoupled behind stable, small APIs so components can be tested, replaced, or
    examined in isolation.
  \item \textbf{Proof-carrying answers:} Every verdict is paired with a compact,
    serializable proof object that records the arguments, attacks, defeat decisions,
    and provenance metadata used to reach that verdict.
  \item \textbf{Deterministic, auditable traces:} The system emits canonicalized
    traces (with signature checks) so replay and determinism checks are straightforward.
\end{enumerate}

These principles prioritize explainability and reproducibility over raw scale or
natural-language coverage: the goal is to make reasoning artifacts defensible and
amenable to human review.

\section{System Architecture and Semantics}
Episteme implements three main modules:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Manas (Parser):} Converts natural language inputs into \emph{BeliefProposal}
    objects. Manas performs tokenization, predicate normalization, and entity canonicalization.
  \item \textbf{Chitta (Memory):} Stores beliefs with metadata fields: \texttt{rank},
    \texttt{activation}, \texttt{confidence}, and \texttt{provenance}. Chitta exposes a
    deterministic API for adding proposals and retrieving relevant beliefs for a query.
  \item \textbf{Buddhi (Reasoner):} Builds structured arguments from Chitta's beliefs,
    identifies attacks, and resolves defeat using a lexicographic comparison ordered by
    \emph{specificity}, \emph{source reliability}, \emph{activation}, \emph{epistemic rank}, and \emph{recency}.
\end{itemize}

Formally, a belief is a tuple
\[ b = \langle\mathrm{id},\;\ell,\;r,\;a,\;c,\;p\rangle \]
where $\ell$ is a ground literal (atomic predicate or its negation), $r\in\mathbb{Z}$ is
the epistemic rank, $a\in[0,1]$ the activation, $c\in[0,1]$ the confidence score, and
$p$ a provenance token. An argument is a finite structure
\[ A = (\kappa, S, \phi)\]
where $\kappa$ is the claim literal, $S\subseteq\mathcal{B}$ is the finite supporting set
of beliefs (with $\mathcal{B}$ the current belief base), and $\phi(A)$ is the attribute
vector used for defeat comparisons:
\[\phi(A) = (\mathrm{spec}(A),\;\mathrm{src}(A),\;a(A),\;r(A),\;\mathrm{rec}(A)),\]
where $\mathrm{spec}(A)$ denotes specificity (higher for more specific rules),
$\mathrm{src}(A)$ denotes a source-reliability score, and $\mathrm{rec}(A)$ denotes recency.

Define the attack relation $\mathit{Att}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}$ on the
set of arguments $\mathcal{A}$ by
\[ (A,B)\in\mathit{Att} \iff \kappa_A = \neg\kappa_B \;\vee\; A\text{ derives a literal that contradicts }B.\]

Defeat is resolved by the lexicographic order on attribute vectors. For two arguments
$A,B\in\mathcal{A}$, write
\[ A \succ_{\mathrm{lex}} B \quad\text{iff}\quad \phi(A) >_{\mathrm{lex}} \phi(B),\]
meaning the first non-equal component of $\phi(A)$ and $\phi(B)$ is larger for $A$.
If neither $A\succ_{\mathrm{lex}} B$ nor $B\succ_{\mathrm{lex}} A$ holds, the pair is
declared a \texttt{CONFLICT} and marked unresolved.

\section{Implementation Overview}
This section documents concrete design and implementation choices made in Episteme.

\subsection{Component APIs}
The system exposes three concise interfaces:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Parser API:} A function that accepts raw input (string or structured)
    and returns zero or more \emph{BeliefProposal} objects. A BeliefProposal contains
    a canonical literal, optional supporting metadata (source, reliability), and a
    suggested rank/activation value. The parser is intentionally pluggable.
  \item \textbf{Memory API:} Deterministic operations for adding proposals, listing
    relevant beliefs for a query, and retrieving provenance. Chitta stores beliefs
    as compact JSON objects with fields like \texttt{rank}, \texttt{activation},
    \texttt{confidence}, and \texttt{provenance}.
  \item \textbf{Reasoner API:} Accepts a query and a belief-set, constructs candidate
    arguments, computes attack relations, and returns a tuple (verdict, proof).
    The proof is a structured JSON object recording argument nodes, supporting
    beliefs, attack edges, and the outcome of lexicographic comparisons.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Proof Format and Trace Canonicalization}
Proof objects are deliberately compact and deterministic. Each proof contains:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A list of \emph{argument} entries; each entry references supporting belief IDs.
  \item An \emph{attack} adjacency list describing which arguments attack which.
  \item The lexicographic comparison decisions for each pairwise conflict (which fields
    were compared and the chosen winner or tie).
  \item Metadata: timestamps, prover version, and a canonical trace signature (SHA-1
    over a canonical JSON serialization).
\end{itemize}

Canonicalization normalizes field order, string casing for predicates, and a stable
serialization to ensure that identical reasoning runs produce identical trace
signatures (modulo non-deterministic external factors, which are avoided by design).

\section{Formal Properties and Pseudocode}
\label{sec:formal}

\subsection{Reasoner pseudocode}
The core reasoner operates by constructing candidate arguments from a finite
belief base, computing pairwise attacks, and resolving defeat via lexicographic
comparison. Pseudocode for the high-level procedure follows.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Reasoner: Build-and-Compare}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Procedure{Reason}{Query $q$, BeliefBase $B$}
  \State $\mathcal{A} \gets$ BuildArguments($q,B$)
  \State $\mathit{Att} \gets$ ComputeAttacks($\mathcal{A}$)
  \ForAll{$(A,B)\in\mathit{Att}$}
    \If{$\phi(A) >_{\mathrm{lex}} \phi(B)$}
      \State mark $A$ defeats $B$
    \ElsIf{$\phi(B) >_{\mathrm{lex}} \phi(A)$}
      \State mark $B$ defeats $A$
    \Else
      \State mark conflict unresolved
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State $P \gets$ ConstructProof($\mathcal{A},\mathit{Att}$,comparisons)
  \State \Return (Verdict($P$), $P$)
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Formal properties}
The following section gives more rigorous lemmas and proofs, presents a complexity
analysis of the core procedures, and states a correctness theorem that relates
Episteme's operational proofs to a standard notion of defeasible entailment.

\begin{lemma}[Canonicalization invariance]
Let $P_1,P_2$ be two proof objects that enumerate the same multiset of argument
entries, attack edges, and pairwise comparison outcomes. Under the canonicalization
function $\mathit{canon}(\cdot)$ used by Episteme, $\mathit{canon}(P_1)=\mathit{canon}(P_2)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The canonicalization procedure deterministically sorts argument entries by their
identifier ordering, normalizes string casings for predicate names, orders adjacency
lists lexicographically, and emits numerics with fixed precision. Since $P_1$ and
$P_2$ have identical multisets of elements, each normalization step produces the
same ordered sequence; hence the serializations are identical.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Deterministic argument construction]
Assume the argument builder enumerates candidate supports by a fixed, deterministic
iteration over the belief base $\mathcal{B}$. Then the set $\mathcal{A}$ of
constructed arguments (with their internal support sets) is unique.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By hypothesis the builder applies a deterministic rule application order and a
deterministic enumeration of belief combinations (for example, lexicographic order
on belief identifiers). Therefore every rule-triggering combination is considered
iff it meets the rule preconditions, and is considered in the same sequence across
runs; consequently the resulting set $\mathcal{A}$ is invariant.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Determinism]
Given a fixed belief base $\mathcal{B}$ and a reasoner implementation that uses the
deterministic construction and canonicalization procedures above, the resulting
proof object $P$ and its trace signature $\mathit{sig}(P)$ are unique and
invariant across runs.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Combine Lemmas 1--2. Deterministic argument construction yields a unique $\mathcal{A}$;
attack detection is a deterministic predicate on pairs in $\mathcal{A}$; pairwise
comparisons use numeric vectors and a fixed lexicographic comparison. Thus the
raw proof record is unique. Applying the deterministic canonicalization function
to this unique record yields a unique serialization and signature.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Termination]
If the belief base $\mathcal{B}$ is finite and each rule application inspects at most
$s$ beliefs (arity bounded by $s$), then the reasoner terminates after a finite
number of steps.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Argument construction considers at most $\sum_{i=1}^{s}\binom{|\mathcal{B}|}{i}$
candidate supports; attack computation inspects at most $\binom{m}{2}$ pairs where
$m$ is the number of constructed arguments; each lexicographic comparison is finite
cost. All these bounds are finite when $\mathcal{B}$ is finite, so the overall
procedure terminates.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Complexity analysis}
Let $n=|\mathcal{B}|$ and suppose rule arity is bounded by $s\ge 1$. Let $R$ be the
number of rule schemas and assume each rule produces at most $\alpha$ candidates per
eligible support set. Then the number of arguments $m$ satisfies
\[ m = O\big(R\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{s}\binom{n}{i}\big) = O(R\cdot n^{s}).\]
Building arguments therefore costs $O(m\cdot C_{build})$ where $C_{build}$ is the
per-argument construction cost. Computing attacks requires $O(m^{2}\cdot C_{att})$
time where $C_{att}$ is the cost to test whether $A$ attacks $B$ (typically linear
in support size). Pairwise comparisons cost $O(m^{2}\cdot k)$ where $k$ is the fixed
dimension of the attribute vector $\phi(\cdot)$. Hence the overall worst-case time is
\[ O\big(m^{2}\cdot (C_{att}+k) + m\cdot C_{build}\big) = O\big(R^{2} n^{2s}\big)\]
under coarse-grained assumptions; in practice pruning, bounded rule arity, and
indexing reduce the observed cost substantially. These bounds are worst-case
asymptotic estimates; this paper does not empirically validate scaling behavior
for large belief bases. Empirical complexity evaluation is left to future work
and is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:future}.

\subsection{Correctness relative to a defeasible theory}
To relate operational proofs to a formal defeasible logic, consider a simple
defeasible theory $\mathcal{D}=(F,R_d,>)$ where $F$ is a finite set of facts, $R_d$
is a finite set of defeasible rules of the form $\rho: A_1,\ldots,A_k \Rightarrow C$,
and $>$ is a superiority relation between rules. A standard argumentation-based
proof theory for $\mathcal{D}$ accepts a literal $\ell$ iff there exists an
undefeated argument for $\ell$ under the superiority relation.

\begin{proposition}[Informal soundness correspondence]
If Episteme returns a verdict accepting $\ell$ (i.e. there exists an undefeated
constructed argument $A$ with claim $\ell$), then, under the intended mapping of
Episteme's beliefs and rules to a defeasible theory $\mathcal{D}$, $\ell$ is
accepted by the corresponding argumentation proof theory. This statement is an
operational correspondence and is presented here as an informal proof sketch.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Map Episteme's belief base and rule set to $F$ and $R_d$ preserving literals and
rule antecedents. The constructed argument $A$ corresponds to a tree of rule
applications in the defeasible theory. Because $A$ is undefeated by construction
(no opponent argument strictly wins under the lexicographic comparator) and the
comparator realizes a deterministic priority among conflicting arguments, the
standard argumentation proof theory will accept $\ell$ under the mapped theory.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Conditional correspondence (proof sketch)]
Under the additional modeling assumptions below (faithful encoding of rules,
comparator correspondence, and exhaustive exploration up to arity $s$), any literal
accepted in the defeasible proof theory of $\mathcal{D}$ will be produced by Episteme
as an undefeated constructed argument. The statement is conditional and presented as
an informal proof sketch rather than a full metatheoretic completeness result.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
If each defeasible derivation in $\mathcal{D}$ can be represented by a corresponding
argument in Episteme and the lexicographic comparator implements the same priority
ordering as the superiority relation $>$, then any defeasible proof's defeaters are
mirrored by the reasoner's defeated arguments. Thus an accepted proof in $\mathcal{D}$
corresponds to an undefeated constructed argument in Episteme under these conditions.
\end{proof}

These correctness statements clarify which aspects are implementation-robust
(soundness, determinism, termination) and which require stronger modeling
assumptions to obtain completeness (faithful rule encoding and comparator
correspondence). They provide a basis for rigorous reviewer evaluation and for
future formal refinements.

\section{Example: Building and Recording a Decision}
To illustrate how the system behaves in practice, consider a minimal example pipeline
that answers the query "Can Tweety fly?" given a set of beliefs about class membership
and exceptions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The parser receives the NL sentence "Tweety is a bird" and emits a BeliefProposal
    with literal \texttt{bird(tweety)} and suggested rank 10 with provenance "input:1".
  \item A separate proposal encodes "Tweety is a penguin" with rank 12 (more specific).
  \item Chitta stores both beliefs with deterministic identifiers and provenance.
  \item The reasoner constructs two candidate arguments: one supporting \texttt{can\_fly(tweety)}
    from default bird inheritance, and one defeating that claim due to penguin exception.
  \item Pairwise lexicographic comparison uses specificity (penguin is more specific),
    source reliability, and rank; the defeat decision records which fields caused the
    penguin-derived argument to defeat the bird-derived argument.
  \item The reasoner returns verdict \texttt{no} (cannot fly) plus a proof object that
    lists the two arguments, the attack edge, and the comparison outcome (specificity -> winner).
\end{enumerate}

The proof JSON for this case is compact and includes: argument nodes with belief IDs,
attack edges, and a \texttt{comparison} object that shows the lexicographic steps.

\section{Implementation Notes}
The prototype is implemented in Python with a focus on clarity and reproducibility rather
than raw performance. Key pragmatic choices:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Use of small, explicit data structures (JSON-serializable) for beliefs and proofs.
  \item Deterministic iteration orders and canonical JSON serialization for trace stability.
  \item A pluggable parser interface so future work can swap in statistical or neural
    parsers while preserving the proof-carrying and trace formats.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Terminology}
To avoid ambiguity we use the following terms consistently throughout the paper:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Grounding:} the process of mapping parser outputs (surface forms)
    to canonical belief representations stored in the memory layer. Grounding failures
    are cases where no suitable canonical belief or anchor is found for a parsed item.
  \item \textbf{Canonicalization:} deterministic normalization applied to proofs and
    belief records (field ordering, predicate casing, stable serialization) used to
    compute trace signatures for replay checks.
  \item \textbf{Parsing / Parser:} the NL front-end that produces \emph{BeliefProposal}
    objects from natural-language input; parsing is distinct from grounding (mapping)
    and from subsequent argument construction.
  \item \textbf{Argument construction:} forming candidate arguments from grounded
    beliefs (collections of premises and an inferred conclusion) used by the reasoner.
  \item \textbf{Reasoning:} the process of computing attack relations among arguments
    and producing a verdict plus a proof object that explains the decision.
  \item \textbf{Defeat ordering (lexicographic):} the operational tie-breaking policy
    used by the reasoner when two arguments conflict; fields are compared in a
    deterministic lexicographic order such as (specificity, source
    reliability, activation, rank, recency).
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion and Use Cases}
Episteme is intended as a platform for experimentation, teaching, and reproducible
research on defeasible reasoning. Typical uses:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Research on defeat semantics:} Swap defeat comparison functions while
    keeping proof traces comparable.
  \item \textbf{Teaching and demonstrations:} Show students how arguments and attacks
    are constructed and decided in a transparent manner.
  \item \textbf{Audit and forensics:} When a downstream decision matters, auditors can
    inspect the proof object and provenance to verify the reasoning steps.
\end{itemize}

The architecture is intentionally conservative: rather than promising broad, open-domain
NLP performance, Episteme gives researchers a small, auditable substrate on which to
experiment with semantics, provenance policies, and canonical trace formats.

\section{Evaluation and Failure Localization}
The central empirical claim of this work is not that Episteme achieves high end-to-end
accuracy on natural-language reasoning. The stronger and more defensible claim is that
the split pipeline allows measurable, execution-backed failure localization.

Concretely, controlled symbolic evaluation remains decisive, while integrated natural-
language evaluation collapses primarily at grounding. This directly supports the thesis
that representation alignment is currently the dominant bottleneck, not the defeat
ordering alone.

  localized instability (3 nondeterministic replay cases) and legitimate unresolved ties
  (3 defeat-level conflicts).

This decomposition is the key experimental narrative of the paper: under controlled
symbolic grounding the reasoning core is capable of producing decisive verdicts,
while integrated NL runs expose an upstream alignment bottleneck.

\section{Limitations}
This project intentionally limits scope. Specific constraints:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The parser uses a pattern-based mock-up (Manas) rather than a production NLP stack.
  \item The benchmarks are deliberately small and adversarial; results do not generalize
    to broad open-domain QA without further work.
  \item The reasoner implements a fixed lexicographic defeat ordering; this is a
    reasonable operational choice, but not yet a fully justified theoretical semantics.
  \item The current evaluation is still early-stage and should be expanded into more
    systematic category-wise tables and controlled symbolic comparisons.
\end{itemize}

\section{Threats to Validity}
We highlight several threats to internal and external validity and explain mitigations
where applicable.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Small benchmark scale:} the benchmarks are intentionally compact and
    adversarial. This limits external validity; results should be seen as diagnostic
    rather than comprehensive. We mitigate this by releasing runner scripts and
    encouraging replication on larger suites.
  \item \textbf{Handcrafted parser and grounding heuristics:} Manas is a pattern-based
    prototype. Grounding failures may partly reflect parser design choices. We therefore
    separate parsing, grounding, and reasoning metrics so that grounding-specific
    failures are not conflated with reasoning behavior.
  \item \textbf{Deterministic defeat ordering:} the lexicographic ordering is an
    operational decision. Different orderings may change verdict statistics; we
    recommend ablation studies (see Future Work) to quantify sensitivity.
  \item \textbf{Measurement and labeling bias:} failure labels are emitted by the
    pipeline and augmented by simple heuristics. While emission at execution time
    improves fidelity, labeling heuristics can be improved; we publish raw traces so
    others can reclassify outcomes under alternate taxonomies.
  \item \textbf{Replay nondeterminism:} a small number of nondeterministic replay
    cases were observed. These are treated as diagnostic signals; further engineering
    (stable IDs, canonical random seeds) can reduce such occurrences.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
The next version of the system should strengthen three areas.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Benchmarking:} add larger symbolic suites, category-wise metrics, and
    clearer separation of parser, grounding, and reasoning failures.
  \item \textbf{Semantics:} compare the current lexicographic defeat ordering against
    alternative formalizations and justify why one ordering should be preferred in a
    given application setting.
  \item \textbf{Parsing front ends:} replace the current primitive parser with a more
    reliable NLP or neuro-symbolic front end while preserving the proof-carrying core.
  \item \textbf{Ablation studies:} conduct targeted ablations of defeat ordering and
    replay policies. Example ablations include reordering lexicographic fields
    (e.g., swap specificity and source reliability), removing a field from the
    comparison, or replacing deterministic tie-breaking with a randomized policy;
    measure how these changes affect decisive verdicts, conflict rates, and proof
    stability. Similarly, vary grounding thresholds or anchor-matching heuristics to
    quantify sensitivity of end-to-end outcomes to parser/grounding choices. Even if
    small in scope, such controlled ablations would clarify which operational choices
    drive verdict changes and strengthen the empirical claims.
\end{itemize}

These are natural extensions, but they are not required to understand the present paper.
The current contribution stands on its own as a transparent system design and invites
further work on improved grounding and rigorous ablations.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented Episteme, a systems-focused attempt to make parsing, grounding, and reasoning
transparent and auditable through proof-carrying traces and a split evaluation protocol.
Results from initial adversarial NL runs show that most failures are upstream of the
reasoner; the symbolic runner demonstrates that the reasoning core behaves as designed
on canonical inputs. Artifacts and scripts have been released to support reproducibility and
to encourage further work on improved grounding and rigorous ablations.
